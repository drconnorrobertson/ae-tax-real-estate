Build a timeline for each asset and phase

A closing date, paid invoice and first rent receipt record different events. Use this calendar to connect each event to the property, unit or component it actually concerns. Keep the original documents and identify what they establish before asking the preparer to determine a reporting date.

For residential rentals, IRS Publication 527, under Placed in Service, describes readiness and availability for the intended rental use. It also gives examples where purchase, installation and first occupancy occur at different times. The timeline below is an original fictional record exercise. It does not determine a depreciation deduction or extend residential guidance to every commercial installation.

A fictional duplex with a separate appliance installation

Assume an owner acquires a duplex in Year 1. Unit A already has a tenant. Unit B needs work before being offered for rent. A replacement appliance for Unit B is purchased during that work but installed later. No event in this exercise supplies a complete tax conclusion for the whole building.

The June 10 payment does not document completion. The August 1 lease commencement does not erase evidence of an earlier availability date. The appliance needs its own record rather than inheriting the building closing date. Ask the reviewer to assess whether the July 8 evidence is complete and whether unresolved work affected the intended use. Do not select a date solely because it produces a preferred tax year.

Record what an apparent contradiction means

If the manager's release says July 8 but the contractor identifies unfinished essential work through July 12, keep both records. Ask which work remained and whether the release described the same unit and scope. A later invoice could bill earlier completed work; a dated listing could advertise a future availability date. Read the underlying event rather than treating every document date as interchangeable.

For phased work, create separate entries for the unit, shared system and later component. Note dependencies: a finished room may still depend on a system or approval that is not ready. Conversely, work in one unit does not establish the status of every other unit. This calendar records facts for review and does not calculate a building-wide depreciation start date.

Adapt the evidence to the property

  • Occupied and phased rentals: separate continuing operations, vacant units and newly completed components. Retain unit-level work and availability records.
  • Tenant and owner improvements: identify the payer, owner, lease responsibility and actual installation scope. A practice's operating date does not alone establish the readiness or ownership of each installation.
  • Commercial systems and equipment: preserve commissioning, intended use and dependencies. Obtain the guidance applicable to the asset and activity rather than assuming a residential rental example determines its treatment.
  • Seasonal or short-term rentals: separate an advertised future opening from actual availability. Keep dated operating records and investigate any permit, access or essential-work issue affecting the intended use.

Deliver the event register with the underlying evidence, cost history and ownership information. Ask the preparer to record the date and scope accepted, facts still needed and any corresponding asset-schedule change. Then match the implemented schedule to that written decision. The calendar remains useful after acquisition: add later replacements, changes in use and planned sale events as separate records rather than overwriting the earlier history.
