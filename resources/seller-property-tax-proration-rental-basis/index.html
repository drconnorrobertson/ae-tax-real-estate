Does a Seller’s Property Tax Proration Go Into Rental Basis?

If you pay real estate taxes the seller owed and the seller does not reimburse you, generally add that amount to property basis. Keep your own tax share separate for review as a possible rental expense.

Worked example

Assume you pay a $4,200 bill at closing. The settlement records support $1,500 as the seller’s obligation and $2,700 as yours. If the seller never reimburses the $1,500, carry it to the acquisition-basis workpaper. Route your $2,700 share to the rental tax-expense review, subject to the property's use and the applicable timing rules. Keep the settlement statement and the actual tax bill together so neither amount gets counted twice.

Records to prepare

  1. Identify the buyer and seller shares using the closing date and the period covered by the bill.
  2. Tie reimbursement entries to the seller credit or payment. Separate amounts held in escrow.
  3. Send the reconciled amounts to whoever prepares the land and building allocation and the rental expense schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

What changes if I pay the seller’s share of the real estate tax?

If you pay real estate taxes the seller owed and the seller does not reimburse you, generally add that amount to property basis. Keep your own tax share separate for review as a possible rental expense.

What if I reimburse the seller for the buyer’s share?

If the seller already paid your share and you reimburse the seller, you can usually deduct the amount as a tax expense in the year of purchase. Keep it out of the acquisition basis calculation.

Does a tax escrow deposit belong in the same calculation?

Track escrow funding separately and verify when funds are paid to the taxing authority. Funding the escrow does not by itself establish a tax expense or a property acquisition cost.
